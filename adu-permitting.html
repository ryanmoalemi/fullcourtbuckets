San Diego ADU permits

ADU permitting built around the actual property, jurisdiction and review path.

ADU permits in San Diego are not a single countywide process. The correct path depends on the local agency, zoning, coastal status, fire hazard area, existing structures, utilities, drainage, grading and whether the project is detached, attached, converted space or a JADU.

Permit required The City of San Diego states that a building permit is required to create an ADU or JADU.
Jurisdiction controls the process City of San Diego, unincorporated County and incorporated cities use different portals, forms and local standards.
Site-specific review still matters Preapproved plans do not eliminate zoning, utility, fire, drainage, foundation or site-plan review.

What an ADU permit actually approves

An ADU permit is the agency’s approval of a specific design on a specific parcel. It confirms that the proposed work satisfies the applicable planning, building, structural, energy, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, fire and site requirements shown in the approved construction documents.

The permit does not begin with architectural styling. It begins with the legal parcel, local jurisdiction, zoning designation, existing development, property lines, easements, utility routes, fire conditions and the physical area available for construction.

A plan that works on a flat Clairemont lot may fail on a canyon lot in Mission Hills, a coastal parcel in Pacific Beach, a septic property in Ramona or a multifamily property near a transit corridor. The permit set has to describe the actual conditions accurately.

Step one: identify the agency that has permit authority

A San Diego mailing address does not confirm that the City of San Diego will issue the permit. San Diego County contains the City of San Diego, 17 other incorporated cities and large unincorporated areas. The property’s jurisdiction determines the application portal, zoning standards, fee schedule, fire authority and review staff.

City jurisdiction

City of San Diego

Includes neighborhoods such as North Park, Clairemont, Mira Mesa, La Jolla, Pacific Beach, Point Loma, University City, San Carlos, Tierrasanta, Rancho Peñasquitos and many others. Applications are handled through the City’s Development Services Department.

County jurisdiction

Unincorporated San Diego County

Includes many properties in Ramona, Lakeside, Alpine, Fallbrook, Valley Center, Spring Valley, Bonita, Jamul and other communities. Septic, wells, grading, fire access and rural site conditions may become primary feasibility issues.

Separate local agencies

Incorporated cities

Chula Vista, Encinitas, Carlsbad, Oceanside, Escondido, La Mesa, El Cajon, Poway, Santee and other cities operate their own permitting systems. Do not submit a City of San Diego package to the wrong agency.

Direct check: Use the parcel address and assessor parcel number to confirm the city or unincorporated County jurisdiction before ordering plans, a survey or engineering.

The San Diego ADU permit process

The sequence below is the practical path for most ADU projects. Individual agencies may name the steps differently, but skipping early property work usually creates avoidable redesign and plan-check comments.

Property and records review

Confirm ownership, parcel boundaries, jurisdiction, zoning, overlays, existing permits, assessor records, legal dwelling count and whether prior additions, garages or conversions appear to be unpermitted.

ADU feasibility analysis

Map setbacks, easements, structure locations, access, grade, drainage, fire hazard area, coastal status, parking conditions, sewer or septic, water service and electrical capacity. Establish the realistic buildable envelope before design. See our dedicated ADU feasibility studies guide.

Concept design and utility strategy

Select detached ADU, attached ADU, garage conversion or JADU scope. Set the unit size, bedroom count, entry, windows, private outdoor area, wet-wall location, HVAC, water heating, laundry and utility routes.

Construction documents and engineering

Produce the site plan, architectural drawings, structural design, energy documents, mechanical, electrical and plumbing information, drainage notes and supporting calculations required by the agency.

Online permit application

Upload the application, plans and supporting documents through the correct local portal. Pay intake or review fees and respond quickly to completeness requests.

Plan check and corrections

Reviewers may issue comments from planning, building, structural, fire, transportation, stormwater, utilities or other disciplines. The design team revises the plans and submits a coordinated response.

Permit issuance

After all reviews are cleared, final fees and any required forms are completed. The issued permit, approved plans and inspection record must be available for construction.

Inspections and final approval

Work is inspected in stages. The ADU should not be occupied until required inspections are approved and the jurisdiction issues final approval or the applicable occupancy documentation.

Plans and documents commonly required

Exact submittal requirements vary by jurisdiction and scope. A new detached ADU normally requires a more complete package than a straightforward interior JADU conversion.

  • Permit application and project scope
  • Existing and proposed site plans
  • Property lines, setbacks and easements
  • Existing and proposed floor plans
  • Roof plan and exterior elevations
  • Building and wall sections
  • Foundation and framing plans
  • Structural calculations and details
  • Title 24 energy compliance documents
  • Electrical and load information
  • Plumbing and sewer layout
  • Mechanical and ventilation notes
  • Drainage and stormwater information
  • Fire-zone details when applicable
  • Photos and records requested by the agency
  • Owner, consultant and contractor declarations

Reviews that can affect a San Diego ADU permit

Not every project receives every review. The items below identify the disciplines that commonly control scope, cost or timing.

Review area What reviewers verify San Diego-specific risk
Planning and zoning ADU type, unit count, height, setbacks, lot coverage, front-yard conditions, parking and overlays. Local rules differ among the City, County and incorporated cities. Coastal areas can use a separate approval path.
Building and structural Foundation, framing, lateral design, egress, fire separation, ceiling height, room dimensions and code details. Older garages, hillside sites, expansive soil, retaining walls and irregular additions increase engineering work.
Fire and life safety Fire access, addressing, separation, alarms, vents, glazing, roofing, sprinklers when applicable and hazard-zone requirements. Canyon, slope and wildland-interface properties should be screened before the floor plan is finalized.
Energy and solar Title 24 envelope, windows, HVAC, water heating, ventilation, lighting and solar requirements for applicable new construction. Roof orientation, shading and existing electrical capacity can affect the design and service upgrade scope.
Water and sewer Connection points, pipe sizing, fixture load, backflow or cleanout details, sewer slope and agency requirements. Long sewer runs, low backyard elevations, private laterals, septic systems and utility-district boundaries can change feasibility.
Drainage and grading Runoff, downspout discharge, impervious area, erosion control, grading, retaining walls and protection of adjacent property. Small infill lots, terraced yards and canyon edges leave little room to correct drainage after design.
Coastal review Whether the parcel is in the Coastal Overlay Zone and whether a coastal development approval or special process applies. Beach communities should not assume the inland City process applies without a coastal check.
Historic resources Potential effects on designated or potentially historic structures and districts. Older neighborhoods may require additional records or design coordination even for rear-yard work.

ADU permit fees: what changes the total

There is no reliable single “San Diego ADU permit fee” because the total depends on the issuing agency, project valuation, unit size, review disciplines, utility work, school-fee rules, impact-fee rules and whether additional permits are required.

Agency charges

Application, plan check and permit fees

Building, planning, structural, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, fire and records charges may appear as separate line items. Resubmittals or scope changes can add review time and cost.

Infrastructure

Utility and service work

Electrical panel or service upgrades, new meters, sewer work, water service, trenching and utility-agency requirements are separate from basic building-plan review.

Special conditions

Site-specific permits and studies

Grading, retaining walls, right-of-way work, coastal review, septic evaluation, geotechnical work, stormwater measures or fire requirements can create additional fees.

Budget rule: Separate design and engineering fees, government permit fees, utility charges and construction costs. Combining them into one allowance makes early comparisons misleading.

Permit timing and correction cycles

California law generally requires a local agency to approve or deny a complete ADU application within 60 days. This does not mean every project receives an issued construction permit exactly 60 calendar days after the first upload. An incomplete package, applicant-requested change, outside-agency review or unresolved correction can extend the real schedule.

Qualifying applications using preapproved ADU plans are subject to a shorter state review period, but the site plan, utilities, foundation, fire conditions and local development standards still have to be resolved.

What usually slows an ADU permit

  • Submitting before jurisdiction, zoning or coastal status is confirmed.
  • Plans that conflict with the survey, assessor records or existing construction.
  • Missing structural calculations, energy documents or application forms.
  • Changing size, layout, windows or utilities during plan check.
  • Unresolved sewer, septic, electrical, drainage or fire-access issues.
  • Corrections answered by one consultant but not coordinated across the full set.

ADU inspections after the permit is issued

Approved plans authorize construction, but the work must still pass field inspections. Inspection names and sequence vary by project and jurisdiction.

Before concrete or concealment

Typical checks include excavation, footing dimensions, reinforcing steel, underground plumbing, sewer, electrical conduit, grounding and under-slab work.

Framing and rough systems

Inspectors review framing, shear walls, hold-downs, roof structure, windows, rough electrical, rough plumbing, mechanical work, fire blocking and required corrections before insulation and drywall.

Final approval

Final inspection covers completed life-safety items, fixtures, equipment, exterior work, drainage, addressing, alarms, energy measures and correction closeout. Utility releases may be coordinated separately.

Field rule: Build from the stamped approved plans. Changes made in the field may require a formal construction change or revised permit documents before the inspector can approve the work.

Preapproved ADU plans: useful, but not a permit shortcut for every lot

The City of San Diego accepts specified preapproved ADU plans. State law provides a shorter review period for a complete application using a local agency's preapproved plan. The owner still needs a site-specific package. See our complete guide to pre-approved ADU plans in San Diego.

What the standard plan may solve

  • Basic architectural layout and elevations
  • Pre-reviewed structural approach within stated limits
  • Repeatable code details
  • Reduced design time for owners who accept the standard layout

What remains site-specific

  • Property survey and site plan
  • Setbacks, height and fire-zone conditions
  • Foundation adaptation and soils conditions
  • Sewer, water, electrical and drainage routes
  • Coastal, grading, septic or right-of-way requirements
  • Plan licensing or provider terms when applicable

San Diego ADU permit questions

Do I need a building permit for an ADU or JADU in the City of San Diego?

Yes. The City states that a building permit is required to create an ADU or JADU. The issued permit type depends on the project and the number of dwelling units being created.

Can an ADU permit be denied because a neighbor objects?

A code-compliant ADU is generally reviewed ministerially under state law rather than through a discretionary neighborhood approval process. The application still must satisfy objective standards and applicable health and safety requirements.

Does a garage conversion need plans and inspections?

Yes. Conversion projects require plans that document the existing structure and proposed residential work. Inspectors may require access to verify the slab, framing, electrical, plumbing, fire separation, insulation, ventilation and other concealed conditions.

Can construction begin while the permit is still in plan check?

Do not begin permit-required ADU work before the required permit is issued. Early demolition or construction can create enforcement, redesign, inspection and financing problems.

Is a survey always required?

The required level of survey work depends on the project, agency and site. A survey becomes especially important when the ADU is close to setbacks, property lines, easements, slopes, retaining walls or disputed fence lines.

Who should respond to plan-check comments?

The architect or designer should coordinate the full response with the structural engineer, energy consultant, civil engineer and other specialists. Corrections should be answered consistently across every affected sheet and calculation.
