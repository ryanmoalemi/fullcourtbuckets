ADU Feasibility Studies in San Diego

Find out what the property can support before paying for the wrong ADU design.

An ADU feasibility study checks the issues that determine whether a project is straightforward, expensive, restricted or impractical: jurisdiction, zoning, buildable area, setbacks, easements, existing structures, sewer or septic, electrical capacity, fire conditions, coastal review, drainage, grading, construction access and the intended use of the unit.

A feasibility study is the point where an ADU idea becomes a property-specific decision. It does not start with cabinet colors or a generic 1,200-square-foot plan. It starts with the legal parcel, local agency, existing buildings, site measurements, utility routes and the conditions that can force redesign.

San Diego takeaway: two neighboring properties can have different ADU constraints because of easements, sewer elevation, canyon exposure, historic status, coastal boundaries, utility locations, lot geometry or unpermitted improvements. The address must be reviewed individually.

What an ADU feasibility study actually does

A feasibility study identifies the strongest ADU path for the property and exposes the problems that should be solved before full architectural drawings, engineering and permit fees are committed. It combines desktop research, property records, mapping, owner-provided information and site observations when applicable.

1

Confirms the legal path

The study identifies the responsible jurisdiction, zoning, overlays, existing unit count and whether the project is most likely to proceed as a detached ADU, attached ADU, conversion, JADU or another housing strategy.

2

Defines the usable site

It maps the realistic building area after setbacks, easements, structures, slope, drainage, access, utilities, trees, retaining walls, patios, pools and fire conditions are considered.

3

Identifies budget risks

It flags conditions that can materially change cost, such as long sewer runs, pumping, panel upgrades, retaining walls, septic work, demolition, limited access, coastal review and special foundations.

When a feasibility study is most valuable

Every ADU benefits from early feasibility, but the study becomes especially important when the property is constrained, the owner is comparing multiple concepts or the project budget cannot absorb major surprises.

  • You have not confirmed whether the property is inside a city or unincorporated County.
  • The yard is small, narrow, irregular, sloped, terraced or adjacent to a canyon.
  • The proposed ADU is close to a property line, easement, pool, retaining wall or existing structure.
  • The property is in La Jolla, Pacific Beach, Ocean Beach, Point Loma or another coastal area.
  • The parcel may be inside a high or very high fire hazard area.
  • The property uses septic, a private well or a nonstandard utility arrangement.
  • The existing garage, addition or second unit may not match permit records.
  • You are comparing detached ADU, attached ADU, garage conversion, JADU or second-story options.
  • You are evaluating a property before purchase or making an offer contingent on ADU potential.
  • You need a realistic concept before requesting contractor pricing or financing.

Feasibility should answer one direct question

"What ADU can this property support without creating unnecessary entitlement, utility, construction or operating problems?"

A useful study does not simply say that state law allows ADUs. It explains which option fits the lot, where it can go, what information is still missing, what may require agency confirmation and which costs should be investigated before design.

What we review in a San Diego ADU feasibility study

The scope is adjusted to the property and proposed ADU type. The following categories form the core review.

Review category Questions answered Why it affects the project
Jurisdiction Is the parcel in the City of San Diego, unincorporated County or another incorporated city? The agency determines the code path, forms, permit portal, fees, fire review and local interpretations.
Zoning and overlays What is the base zone? Is the parcel coastal, historic, airport-related, environmentally sensitive or subject to another overlay? Overlay zones can add review, design conditions or separate approvals beyond the basic ADU rules.
Existing development How many legal units and structures exist? Do assessor records, aerials and permit records appear consistent? An unpermitted addition, garage conversion or second unit can affect the site plan and permit strategy.
Buildable envelope Where can an ADU physically fit after property lines, setbacks, easements, structures and site features? The usable area is often smaller than the open yard visible from the house.
Height and massing Does a one-story unit work, or is a second story needed? What privacy, fire or structural consequences follow? Vertical construction can preserve yard area but increase cost, neighbor sensitivity and engineering complexity.
Sewer or septic Where is the likely connection? Can waste flow by gravity? Is the property on septic? Sewer elevation, trench length, pumping or septic capacity can decide whether a layout is practical.
Electrical What service and panel information is visible? Is an all-electric ADU likely to require an upgrade? HVAC, water heating, cooking, laundry, EV charging and solar can place substantial load on older service.
Water What is the likely supply route? Are pressure, meter, district or trenching questions present? Long or obstructed runs affect construction cost and the location of kitchens, bathrooms and laundry.
Fire conditions Is the property in a mapped fire hazard area, near a canyon or subject to brush management concerns? Access, setbacks, vents, glazing, materials, defensible space and sprinklers may require early coordination.
Drainage and grading Where does runoff flow? Will construction require cut, fill, retaining walls or hardscape changes? New roofs and paving can create drainage problems on tight lots, slopes and properties below neighboring grades.
Construction access Can crews, materials, concrete, equipment and debris reach the build area? Narrow side yards, stairs, pools, walls, mature landscaping and occupied homes increase labor and staging cost.
Use and financial fit Is the unit for parents, adult children, long-term rent, guests, downsizing or future flexibility? The intended user should determine size, bedroom count, privacy, accessibility, storage and finish priorities.

Our ADU feasibility study process

The objective is to reach a defensible go, revise or stop decision before the project moves into full design.

Define the owner�s goal

Confirm intended use, preferred ADU type, target size, bedroom count, accessibility needs, privacy goals, rental strategy, schedule and budget range.

Collect parcel and existing-property information

Review the address, APN, ownership information, assessor data, available permits, maps, aerials, owner photos, existing plans, surveys and utility information.

Research jurisdiction, zoning and overlays

Confirm the permit authority, base zone, coastal status, fire mapping and other property-specific regulations that may affect the ADU path.

Map the preliminary buildable area

Locate existing buildings, likely setbacks, access paths, easements, grade changes, retaining walls, drainage routes, utility areas and major site obstacles.

Compare practical ADU options

Test detached, attached, garage conversion, JADU, second-story or alternate layouts against site, privacy, construction and utility constraints.

Identify unresolved verification items

List information that requires a surveyor, engineer, utility provider, septic specialist, title review, fire authority or permitting agency before final design.

Recommend the next design and permit path

Summarize the strongest concept, main risks, likely consultants, agency questions and work that should occur before permit drawings begin. The next step is typically ADU permitting, with costs outlined in our ADU cost guide.

How we classify ADU feasibility risk

Feasibility is not simply �allowed� or �not allowed.� A legal ADU may still be financially weak because of site work, utilities, access or the amount of existing property value it displaces.

A

Low-complexity candidate

Clear jurisdiction, flat build area, practical setbacks, short utility routes, public sewer, good side access, no obvious coastal or fire complication and limited demolition.

B

Workable with verification

The ADU appears practical, but survey, panel, sewer, drainage, structural, fire, title or agency questions should be resolved before the design is finalized.

C

High-complexity candidate

Steep grade, retaining walls, septic limits, pumping, coastal processing, severe access, extensive demolition, uncertain legal structures or several overlapping constraints.

Important: a high-complexity property is not automatically a bad ADU site. It means the owner should obtain targeted verification and realistic pricing before relying on rental projections or generic per-square-foot estimates.

Feasibility means comparing the right ADU type

A detached unit is not automatically the best answer. The study should compare the options that preserve the most property value while meeting the owner�s actual housing goal.

ADU option Best property conditions Main feasibility questions
Detached ADU Usable yard, separate access, practical utility route and desire for maximum privacy. Foundation, sewer, power, drainage, fire, setbacks, access, yard loss and construction staging.
Attached ADU Main house has a logical expansion side and the addition can connect cleanly to structure and systems. Structural tie-in, roof geometry, fire separation, daylight loss, privacy and impact on the main floor plan.
Garage conversion Garage is permitted, structurally useful, well located and not critical for parking, storage or resale. Slab, ceiling height, moisture, fire separation, driveway, utility path and replacement storage.
JADU Existing single-family house has convertible interior space and the owner wants a compact lower-cost unit. Owner occupancy, shared sanitation where permitted, fire separation, egress, privacy and loss of main-house space.
Second-story ADU Ground area is limited but an existing structure or new stacked design may support vertical development. Foundation and framing capacity, stairs, height, fire access, privacy, construction disruption and cost.
Multifamily ADU strategy Existing multifamily properties with qualifying non-livable areas or room for detached units. Existing legal unit count, parking, fire access, utility load, open-space impacts and state-law unit allowances.

San Diego ADU feasibility warning signs

These conditions do not automatically prevent an ADU. They indicate that targeted research or professional verification should happen before a final layout and budget are accepted.

  • The yard slopes away from the public sewer or existing lateral.
  • A canyon, open-space edge or dense brush is close to the proposed building area.
  • The property is in the Coastal Overlay Zone or near an appealable coastal area.
  • Fences, walls or improvements do not align with available parcel mapping.
  • A recorded easement crosses the only obvious building location.
  • The side-yard path is blocked by stairs, HVAC equipment, pools, walls or utility meters.
  • The garage or addition does not appear in permit records.
  • The property relies on septic or a private well.
  • Large retaining walls or substantial grading appear necessary.
  • The electrical service is older or already carries large loads.
  • The ADU would eliminate all private outdoor space for the main house.
  • The only practical windows directly face neighboring or primary-home living spaces.

What feasibility cannot guarantee

An initial study is not a building permit, boundary survey, title opinion, structural calculation, geotechnical report, utility will-serve letter, septic approval or final agency determination.

Conditions hidden underground or inside existing construction may only become known through field work, demolition, testing or detailed engineering. Final approval belongs to the reviewing agencies.

Best practice: treat feasibility as the decision map. It identifies which professional, agency or field verification is worth paying for before the project advances.

Common feasibility mistakes

Using lot size alone

A large lot can have poor ADU feasibility because of slope, septic, easements, fire exposure or long utility runs. A smaller flat lot can be simpler and more profitable.

Buying plans before locating utilities

A kitchen and bathroom on the wrong side of the unit can add trenching, pumping and hardscape restoration that a mirrored or rotated plan could have avoided.

Treating fences as property lines

Fences are not reliable boundary evidence. Tight setback conditions may require survey work before the site plan is finalized.

Ignoring the main home

An ADU can be technically buildable and still damage privacy, daylight, parking, yard use or resale appeal for the primary residence.

Pricing only the building

Site demolition, utilities, drainage, grading, access, retaining walls and upgrades can move the budget more than basic finish selections.

Assuming every issue is visible online

Mapping and records can miss private utilities, undocumented construction, actual elevations, soil conditions and concealed structural problems.

ADU feasibility study FAQs

What information do you need to begin?

Start with the property address, owner goals, preferred ADU type, approximate size, intended use and any available survey, site plan, permit records, utility bills, panel photos, sewer information and property photos.

Can you study a property before I buy it?

Yes. A pre-purchase review can identify likely ADU potential and major risks, but access, records and inspection limitations should be clearly understood. Purchase decisions should not rely on an ADU assumption that has not been verified.

Does a feasibility study include architectural plans?

A feasibility study may include a preliminary site concept or massing recommendation, but it is not the full architectural, structural and permit drawing package unless that scope is separately included.

Will the study tell me the exact construction cost?

It should identify major cost drivers and allow a more informed budget range. Exact pricing requires a defined design, site scope, finish schedule, engineering and contractor bids.

Can a garage conversion be more feasible than a detached ADU?

Yes. A permitted garage with good ceiling height, structure, access and utility proximity may reduce site work. It can also be a poor choice if the garage is structurally deficient, essential for storage or located where residential privacy is weak.

Do I need to contact the City before ordering a feasibility study?

Not necessarily. The study should identify which questions can be answered through published records and which require formal agency confirmation. Complex properties may benefit from a specific preliminary review or agency inquiry.

Can an ADU be feasible legally but not financially?

Yes. A legal project may require expensive retaining walls, pumping, panel upgrades, septic work, coastal processing or severe access labor. Feasibility should evaluate practical cost risk, not only zoning.

What happens after the feasibility study?

The next step is usually targeted verification, concept design, survey or engineering as needed, followed by construction documents and permit submittal. The sequence should follow the risks identified in the study.
