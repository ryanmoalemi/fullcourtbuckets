Attached ADUs in San Diego

Build an attached ADU that feels private, functional and connected to your San Diego home.

An attached accessory dwelling unit can add valuable living space without placing a separate building in the backyard. The new unit may share a wall, floor or roof connection with the primary residence, but it still needs to function as an independent home with its own kitchen, bathroom, sleeping area, entrance, utilities and life-safety systems.

This page is for San Diego homeowners considering a newly constructed ADU that is physically connected to the main residence. What is an attached ADU?

An attached ADU is a complete dwelling unit that is structurally connected to an existing or proposed primary residence. It may be built beside the house, behind it, above an attached garage, above part of the home or as a newly constructed lower-level space. Unlike a converted bedroom or garage, a newly built attached ADU expands beyond the existing building envelope.

The unit typically includes an independent entrance, kitchen, bathroom, living space, sleeping area, storage, heating and cooling, electrical service, plumbing, ventilation, insulation and required smoke and carbon monoxide protection.

1

Side addition ADU

A new unit is constructed along one side of the house. This can create a simple structural connection and efficient utility route when the lot has enough side-yard width.

2

Rear addition ADU

The ADU is added behind the primary home. Rear additions can work well on deeper lots, especially when the unit can receive a private entrance and a small outdoor area.

3

Second-story attached ADU

The unit is constructed above the home or an attached garage. This preserves more yard area but usually introduces greater structural, stair, height, privacy and construction complexity.

4

Attached ADU plus interior reconfiguration

Part of the ADU is new construction and part uses existing space. This hybrid approach can produce a better layout, but it requires careful documentation of existing and proposed work.

Common attached ADU configurations in San Diego

The right configuration depends on lot width, house placement, roof shape, slope, setbacks, parking, outdoor space and how the primary residence is currently used.

Single-story side addition

Often the cleanest option on wider suburban lots. It can provide accessible, stair-free living for parents, caregivers or long-term tenants.

Single-story rear addition

A strong option on deep lots where a rear patio, lawn or underused yard area can be repurposed without crowding the property.

ADU above an attached garage

This can preserve the garage and yard, but the existing foundation, garage walls, framing and lateral system must be evaluated before design assumptions are made.

ADU above part of the home

A second-story addition may fit constrained lots, but stair placement, structural reinforcement, window privacy and construction disruption become major planning items.

Split-level or hillside ADU

Sloped San Diego properties may allow a unit to connect at one level while opening to grade at another. These designs can be attractive but require careful foundation, drainage and retaining-wall review.

Multigenerational family suite

An attached ADU can keep relatives close while preserving separate kitchens, bathrooms, entrances, schedules and day-to-day privacy.

Properties that make strong attached ADU candidates

The strongest candidates are properties where the addition can connect naturally to the house without harming the main residence or forcing expensive structural and site changes.

  • Wide lots with usable side-yard area next to the house.
  • Deep lots with rear space that is not essential to the primary home.
  • Homes with a simple roofline that can accept a clean addition.
  • Properties with logical sewer, water and electrical routes near the proposed unit.
  • Attached garages with structure that may support or be upgraded for an upper-level unit.
  • Corner lots where the ADU can receive a separate entrance from a side street.
  • Homes with an underused wing that can be combined with a modest addition.
  • Properties where a detached ADU would consume too much yard or conflict with access.

Why San Diego owners choose attached ADUs

  • They can preserve more usable backyard area than a detached backyard ADU.
  • Utilities may be easier to extend from the primary residence.
  • They can create convenient housing for parents, adult children or caregivers.
  • They may integrate better with compact or unusually shaped lots.
  • They can share an architectural language with the existing home.
  • They can provide direct proximity without requiring shared interior living space.

Attached ADU challenges San Diego homeowners should catch early

An attached design may look efficient on a site plan but create major problems for the primary home. The feasibility review should consider both residences at the same time.

Loss of windows and natural light

A side or rear addition may cover existing bedroom, kitchen or living-room windows. The main house may need new openings, skylights or an interior reconfiguration to avoid creating dark rooms.

Awkward roof connection

Poorly planned roof valleys, low slopes, mismatched eaves and drainage transitions can make the addition look patched onto the house and increase long-term leak risk.

Structural incompatibility

The existing foundation and framing were not necessarily designed for a new addition or second story. Structural review should happen before the floor plan becomes too fixed.

Shared-wall noise

Bedrooms, televisions, plumbing and laundry placed directly against the shared wall can create constant sound transfer unless the wall and room layout are designed carefully.

No private entrance path

An entrance that passes through the family patio, pool area or bedroom windows may work on paper but feel uncomfortable in everyday use.

Primary-home circulation problems

An addition can block side access, remove a useful exterior door, interrupt the driveway or make trash, gardening and maintenance access more difficult.

Second-story privacy conflicts

Upper-level windows, balconies and exterior stairs can look directly into neighboring yards or the main home's private spaces. Window placement should be deliberate.

Construction disruption

Because the project physically connects to the home, demolition, framing, roof work, utility shutoffs, dust and noise may affect occupants more than detached construction.

Attached ADU rules and code items to verify

ADU standards depend on the local jurisdiction, property type and project design. California law establishes important protections, but the final design must also satisfy applicable building, residential, fire, energy, plumbing, mechanical and electrical requirements.

Item What to check Why it matters
ADU classification Confirm whether the project is a new attached ADU, a conversion or a hybrid addition. The classification can affect size, setbacks, design standards and plan review.
Maximum unit size Review state protections and the local jurisdiction's attached-ADU size standards. The primary home's floor area and proposed bedroom count may affect the permitted size.
Side and rear setbacks Confirm the required distance between new construction and property lines. New additions are reviewed differently from qualifying conversions within existing walls.
Height Verify the allowed wall, plate and overall building height for the specific design. Second-story units, sloped roofs and hillside sites require early height analysis.
Fire separation Determine required assemblies between the ADU, primary residence, garage and property lines. Shared walls, floors, ceilings, openings and penetrations must be detailed correctly.
Structural tie-in Evaluate foundations, shear walls, roof framing and load paths. The addition must work with the existing building without creating structural weakness.
Parking Confirm whether ADU parking is required or exempt and how existing parking is affected. Practical parking still influences tenant use, neighbors and resale.
Utilities Plan sewer, water, gas if used, electrical, HVAC, water heating and metering strategy. Utility capacity and routing are major cost and constructability factors.
Energy compliance Coordinate insulation, glazing, lighting, HVAC, water heating and required energy documentation. Newly constructed space must meet applicable California energy standards.
Stormwater and drainage Review roof runoff, grading, hardscape and drainage around the new foundation. An addition changes how water moves around the home and neighboring properties.

Regulations change and vary across the City of San Diego, incorporated cities and unincorporated San Diego County. Property-specific feasibility should be confirmed before relying on a general size, setback, height or utility assumption.

Attached ADU floor plan strategy

The best attached ADU floor plan solves two homes at once. It creates a comfortable new unit while preserving the light, views, privacy, circulation and useful rooms of the existing home.

Studio attached ADU

A compact studio can work well for a caregiver, relative, office or rental. The plan should use a simple kitchen wall, efficient bathroom and built-in storage to avoid wasted circulation.

One-bedroom attached ADU

A one-bedroom layout usually provides better long-term livability and rental separation. The bedroom should not be placed where shared-wall noise or poor window access reduces comfort.

Two-bedroom attached ADU

Two-bedroom units need stronger storage, laundry, dining and living-space planning. More occupants also increase parking, utility and sound considerations.

Accessible family unit

A single-story attached ADU can be designed with a low-threshold entrance, wider doors, an accessible bathroom and minimal level changes for aging parents or mobility needs.

Layout details that separate a strong attached ADU from a basic addition

  • Place the entrance where occupants can come and go without crossing private family areas.
  • Use closets, bathrooms or storage as sound buffers along shared walls when possible.
  • Keep kitchen and bathroom plumbing near practical utility routes.
  • Protect important windows and natural light in the primary residence.
  • Give the ADU a clear living room rather than an oversized hallway or leftover space.
  • Create a real laundry plan instead of treating it as an afterthought.
  • Plan trash, packages, address visibility, exterior lighting and emergency access.
  • Use windows and landscaping to provide light without creating direct privacy conflicts.
  • Coordinate the roof, eaves, exterior materials and proportions with the original home.
  • Reserve space for mechanical equipment, water heating and electrical panels.

Utilities and building systems

Attached ADUs can benefit from proximity to the main home's systems, but proximity does not guarantee capacity. Existing sewer, water, electrical and HVAC conditions should be checked before the kitchen and bathroom locations are finalized.

Plumbing and sewer

Locate the proposed bathroom, kitchen and laundry in relation to the existing sewer lateral, cleanouts, drain lines and available slope. A short route can reduce trenching, but tying into an old or undersized system may require additional work.

Water service

Confirm service size, pressure, pipe material, shutoff location and the path to the new fixtures. Long runs and difficult crawlspace or slab access can increase cost.

Electrical capacity

Review the main panel, service size, available breaker space and expected loads from cooking, water heating, laundry, HVAC and general circuits. A panel or service upgrade may be needed.

Heating and cooling

Ductless mini-splits are common, but equipment location should be coordinated with windows, walkways, side-yard clearances, neighbor noise and future maintenance.

Water heating

Tank, tankless and heat-pump systems have different electrical, gas, ventilation, condensate and space requirements. The choice should be made early enough to reserve a workable location.

Ventilation

Bathroom exhaust, kitchen ventilation, dryer exhaust and whole-unit ventilation must be routed without interfering with property-line clearances, windows or outdoor living areas.

Attached ADU system checklist

  • Main electrical service and panel capacity
  • ADU subpanel or metering strategy
  • Sewer connection and cleanout location
  • Water service and shutoff plan
  • Kitchen and bathroom exhaust routes
  • Water heater type and location
  • Heating and cooling equipment placement
  • Smoke and carbon monoxide detection
  • Sound and fire treatment at shared assemblies
  • Laundry hookups and dryer strategy
  • Roof drainage and stormwater control
  • Internet, data and exterior lighting

Attached ADU cost drivers in San Diego

Attached ADUs may save money on some utility and site-work items, but the physical connection to the primary residence can add structural, roofing, finish-matching and occupant-protection costs. The price depends on much more than square footage.

Lower-complexity profile

Simple single-story addition, level lot, short utility routes, uncomplicated roof connection, standard finishes, minimal impact on existing rooms and no major service upgrade.

Mid-range profile

Moderate structural work, custom window changes, electrical upgrade, more detailed exterior matching, laundry, upgraded finishes, drainage work and interior modifications to the main home.

Higher-complexity profile

Second-story construction, major foundation reinforcement, hillside work, retaining walls, extensive roof reconstruction, long utility runs, difficult access or substantial remodeling inside the primary residence.

Common attached ADU hidden costs

  • Opening and rebuilding exterior walls where the addition connects.
  • Foundation or framing reinforcement for a second-story unit.
  • Relocating windows, doors, hose bibs, meters, condensers or utility equipment.
  • Reworking roof valleys, gutters, flashing and drainage.
  • Matching older stucco, siding, roofing, trim and paint.
  • Upgrading the electrical panel or utility service.
  • Repairing finishes inside the occupied primary residence.
  • Temporary weather protection during roof and wall tie-in work.
  • Protecting occupants from dust, noise and unsafe construction areas.
  • Correcting unknown conditions exposed after demolition.
  • Creating a private walkway, fence, gate or outdoor area.
  • Modifying patios, driveways, drainage or landscaping around the addition.

Attached ADU permitting and construction process

An attached ADU permit package must document the existing home, the new unit and the structural connection between them. The design should be developed only after the main feasibility risks are understood.

1

Existing-condition review

Measure the home and proposed addition area. Document the roof, foundation, walls, windows, doors, electrical equipment, plumbing, utilities, grading, drainage, driveway, patios and neighboring conditions.

2

Property and code feasibility

Review jurisdiction, lot dimensions, setbacks, height, easements, utility locations, fire access, parking, historic or coastal considerations and whether the proposed configuration qualifies as intended.

3

Concept design

Compare side, rear, upper-level or hybrid options. Test entrances, windows, room dimensions, furniture, utilities, privacy and the effect on the primary residence.

4

Structural and system coordination

Develop the foundation, framing, roof connection, shear system, electrical loads, plumbing routes, mechanical equipment and energy strategy before the drawings are finalized.

5

Permit drawings and submittal

Prepare architectural plans, structural calculations and details, energy documents, specifications and jurisdiction-specific forms for plan review.

6

Construction planning

Establish access, staging, demolition limits, temporary protection, utility shutdowns, inspection sequencing and how the occupied home will remain safe during construction.

7

Construction and inspections

Complete site work, foundation, framing, roof tie-in, rough utilities, exterior enclosure, insulation, drywall, finishes, fixtures and required inspections through final approval.

Where attached ADUs work especially well in San Diego

Attached ADUs can work across San Diego County, but the strongest configuration changes with lot pattern, neighborhood density, terrain and the age of the existing home.

Older urban neighborhoods

North Park, Normal Heights, University Heights, South Park, Golden Hill, City Heights, Kensington and Mission Hills often have compact lots. Rear additions, upper-level units and carefully planned side additions may preserve more outdoor area than detached construction.

Coastal properties

Pacific Beach, Ocean Beach, Point Loma, La Jolla, Encinitas, Solana Beach and Imperial Beach may benefit from high-value attached living space. Coastal review, parking, corrosion, privacy, access and construction logistics require special attention.

Postwar suburban lots

Clairemont, Allied Gardens, San Carlos, Serra Mesa, Mira Mesa, La Mesa, Chula Vista, El Cajon, Santee and Poway often include one-story homes and side or rear yard areas that can support accessible additions.

Hillside and view properties

Mount Soledad, Bay Park, Del Cerro, Encanto, Spring Valley, La Mesa and other sloped areas may support creative lower-level or second-story designs, but foundation, retaining, drainage, access and height become central feasibility issues.

North County family properties

Carlsbad, Oceanside, Vista, San Marcos, Escondido, Encinitas and surrounding communities often have homes where attached ADUs can support parents, adult children or rental income without separating the unit far from the household.

County and semi-rural lots

Ramona, Alpine, Lakeside, Fallbrook, Valley Center and unincorporated areas may offer more space, but septic capacity, wells, fire access, grading and long utility routes can control the project.

Attached ADU mistakes we try to prevent

Designing only from the outside

An addition may fit in the yard but damage the primary home's interior light, circulation or room layout. Both sides of the shared wall must be studied.

Starting with maximum square footage

The largest possible unit is not always the best project. A slightly smaller plan may preserve a patio, window, driveway, view or valuable room in the main house.

Ignoring the roof until late

Roof form affects appearance, drainage, ceiling height, structure and cost. It should be resolved with the floor plan, not after it.

Placing noisy rooms on the shared wall

Back-to-back televisions, headboards, laundry equipment and plumbing can create ongoing conflict between occupants.

Assuming the electrical panel is adequate

Electric cooking, HVAC, laundry and water heating can add substantial demand. Service capacity should be checked before equipment is selected.

Creating a second-story unit without a stair plan

Exterior stairs consume yard and affect privacy. Interior stairs consume floor area and may disrupt the main home. Stair placement should be tested at the beginning.

Forgetting construction access

Tight side yards, occupied homes, pools, slopes and finished landscaping can make material delivery and excavation much harder than the drawings suggest.

Making the ADU look like an obvious add-on

Rooflines, window proportions, exterior materials, trim and massing should make the addition feel intentional while still allowing the unit to have its own identity.

Attached ADU FAQs

What qualifies as an attached ADU?

An attached ADU is a complete independent dwelling that is structurally connected to the primary residence or another qualifying structure. A new side, rear, upper-level or lower-level addition may qualify depending on the design and jurisdiction.

Can an attached ADU have a separate entrance?

Yes. A separate exterior entrance is a central part of making the unit function independently. The entrance path should also provide practical privacy, lighting and emergency access.

Can I build an ADU above my attached garage?

Often, yes, but the existing garage foundation, walls, framing, lateral system, ceiling and fire separation must be evaluated. Reinforcement or partial reconstruction may be required.

Is an attached ADU cheaper than a detached ADU?

It can be, especially when utility routes are short and the structural connection is simple. It can also cost more when the project requires roof reconstruction, structural reinforcement, interior repairs or difficult work around an occupied home.

How large can an attached ADU be?

Maximum size depends on California ADU protections, the local jurisdiction, the primary home's floor area and the proposed bedroom count. A property-specific review is necessary before choosing a final square-footage target.

Can the ADU connect to the main house with an interior door?

The design and code treatment should be confirmed for the specific project. Even when a connection is possible, fire separation, privacy, security and the intended use of the unit should be considered carefully.

Does an attached ADU need separate utilities?

Separate utility service or meters are not always required, but the systems must have adequate capacity and be designed for the local utility and permitting requirements. Owners should also decide how future occupants will divide utility costs.

Can I rent an attached ADU?

A legally permitted attached ADU can generally be used as long-term housing, subject to applicable state law, local regulations, deed restrictions and any property-specific rules.

Will an attached ADU reduce privacy in the main home?

It can if entrances, windows, outdoor areas and shared walls are poorly planned. A strong design uses orientation, landscaping, sound control and room placement to create separation.

Can I live in the main house during construction?

Often, yes, but the answer depends on the scope of demolition, roof work, utility shutdowns and required safety separation. The contractor should establish a clear occupied-home protection plan before work starts.
